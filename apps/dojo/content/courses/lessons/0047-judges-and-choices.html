Code can tell you a hint didn’t leak the solution. It can’t tell you the hint is helpful. For that you use a second, stronger model as a judge, and then you face the real question: why should you believe it? Today you learn the numbers that answer it, the biases to design around, and how to pick the model you ship.

Why this matters

Thursday’s build is the judge on your GPU and a table of two or three models. “I chose the 3B model because it scored within a point of the 7B at a fifth of the latency” is the sentence that separates an AI engineer from someone who called an API.

1. LLM-as-a-judge, done right

An LLM-as-a-judge grades another model’s output against a rubric, a few questions written in advance. Hamel Husain’s method, from real products (Creating an LLM-as-a-judge):

  • One domain expert (here, you) labels a sample first, pass or fail, with a short written critique of each.
  • Binary, not 1-to-5. People read the middle of a scale differently; a yes/no forces the decision and makes agreement measurable.
  • Iterate the judge’s prompt, with your critiques as examples, until it agrees with you. Only then let it grade at scale.
Rubric (each yes / no, as JSON):
  1. Does it name the real cause of the failure?
  2. Would a beginner know where to look next?
  3. Does it avoid giving the fix away?

2. Is the judge any good?

Compare its verdicts with yours on the same hints. With PASS as “positive”:

               you: PASS   you: FAIL
judge: PASS       TP          FP      # FP: a bad hint let through
judge: FAIL       FN          TN      # FN: a good hint rejected

TPR = TP / (TP + FN)        TNR = TN / (TN + FP)
κ   = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)  # po: agreement, pe: chance agreement
  • Agreement alone lies. If 5% of hints are bad, a judge that passes everything agrees 95% of the time and catches nothing.
  • TNR (true negative rate) is the share of bad hints the judge catches: for a gate, the number that matters most.
  • Cohen’s kappa removes the agreement chance alone would produce: 0 is no better than chance, 1 is perfect (Cohen’s kappa).

3. The judge’s biases, and choosing a model

The MT-Bench study measured three biases in judge models (Zheng et al., 2023): position bias (preferring the first answer shown), verbosity bias (preferring longer answers), and self-enhancement bias (preferring its own family’s outputs). It also found strong judges agreeing with humans over 80% of the time, about as often as humans agree with each other. So: swap orders and keep consistent verdicts, cap length with a code rule, and judge with a different model than the one that writes the hints when you can.

Then the choice. Run the same cases on two or three models and record the score and the latency. Report p50 and p95 (nearest rank: sort, take the value at position ⌈p/100 × n⌉), not the mean: one slow hint in twenty is what users remember. Decide the rule before you look: “within 2 points of the best score, the lowest p95 wins”.
