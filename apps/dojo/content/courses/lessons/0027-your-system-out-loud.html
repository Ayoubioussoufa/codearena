1. The shape of the hour

  1. Requirements (a few minutes). What it does (submit, judge, rank) and how well: a verdict in under 10 seconds for most submissions, the availability you promise, and the hard constraint: untrusted code never escapes. Ask; don’t assume.
  2. Estimates. A few numbers that size the design (below).
  3. API and data model. The handful of endpoints and tables everything else hangs on.
  4. The design. Boxes and arrows, drawn while you talk, following one submission end to end.
  5. One part in depth. For you, the judge: it’s where you know more than the interviewer expects.
  6. Scale and trade-offs. What breaks first, what you’d change, and what each choice costs.

Your system-design guide has the vocabulary; worked design #9 is your leaderboard. This lesson is about delivering it.

2. Back of the envelope, out loud

You need four kinds of number, and they’re all multiplication:

  • Rates. 20,000 users × 10 submissions = 200,000 a day. A day has 86,400 seconds, call it 100,000 in your head: about 2 per second on average.
  • Peaks. Traffic isn’t flat. With a peak at 10× the average: about 23 per second.
  • Concurrency, with Little’s law. The number of things in a system equals the rate they arrive times how long each stays (Little’s law). 23 submissions a second, each holding a runner for 6 seconds: about 140 runners busy at once. That one line sizes your worker fleet, and it’s the number most candidates never compute.
  • Storage. 200,000 × 5 KB = 1 GB a day, about 365 GB a year. Not big data: one Postgres holds it. Saying so is as valuable as any diagram.

3. At 100×: name the bottleneck, then the change

Don’t list technologies; follow the load. For CodeArena, in order:

  • Runner capacity breaks first: 140 slots at peak won’t fit next to the API. Runners move to their own hosts, scaled on the queue’s depth, which also shrinks the blast radius of an escape.
  • Database connections: many API and worker processes each holding a pool exhaust Postgres. A pooler (PgBouncer), then read replicas for history pages.
  • Hot reads: the leaderboard at a thousand views a second comes from Redis, with a short cache in front.
  • Isolation strength: at scale, stronger sandboxes (gVisor, Firecracker microVMs) become worth their cost.

Then the trade-offs, each as “X instead of Y, because Z”: BullMQ instead of Kafka, polling rather than WebSockets, one VM instead of Kubernetes, today. And when each would flip.
