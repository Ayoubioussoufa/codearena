RAG sounds like a framework. It’s three ordinary steps and one discipline: find the passages that matter, put them in the prompt, let the model answer, and measure each step separately. Today you build the half that’s plain code, which is also the half where most RAG systems quietly fail.

Why this matters

1. Why retrieve at all

A model knows what was in its training data, frozen at a date, and nothing about your notes, your problems, or yesterday. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fixes that at question time: retrieve the relevant passages from your own store, put them in the prompt, then generate. The original paper combined the model’s own knowledge with a searchable index of documents (Lewis et al., 2020). The answer is then grounded: based on text you can show, not on what the model half-remembers.

Ingestion   notes → chunks → embeddings → store          # offline, idempotent
Query       question → top-k chunks → prompt → model     # per request
Measure     recall@k, MRR (retrieval)  ·  eval score (the answer)

You may not need it. Anthropic’s guidance: under about 200,000 tokens (some 500 pages), just put the whole knowledge base in the prompt, with prompt caching (contextual retrieval). A small local model with a small context is exactly the case where retrieval still earns its place.

2. Ingestion: chunks that make sense alone

  • Chunk on structure, not every N characters: a heading starts a chunk, and a long section splits between paragraphs. A sentence cut in half is retrieved as two useless halves.
  • Each chunk carries its context: repeat the heading line in it, and keep its source. Anthropic’s contextual retrieval goes further and prepends 50–100 tokens explaining where the chunk comes from; with BM25 added, it cut failed retrievals in their tests by 49% (Anthropic).
  • Make it idempotent. Hash every chunk’s text. On each run, insert new chunks, update changed ones, remove deleted ones, and leave the rest: running it twice re-embeds nothing. It’s lesson 14’s idea applied to data.

3. Measure retrieval, then the answer

Write 15 test queries, each labelled with the chunk that should come back. Then:

recall@k = queries whose right chunk is in the top k / all queries
MRR      = average of 1 / rank of the right chunk   (0 if missing)
  • recall@k answers “could the model have seen the answer?” If it’s low, no prompt will save you: fix retrieval first.
  • MRR rewards putting the right chunk first, which matters because models use the start and end of a long context better than the middle (Lost in the middle).
  • Then the hint eval from lesson 46, with and without retrieval, on the same model. Two numbers, one story.

The context block wraps each chunk with its id, in rank order, and stops at the budget without cutting a chunk: the model can cite ids, and a half chunk is worse than none.
