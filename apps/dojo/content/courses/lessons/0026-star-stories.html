You’ve built a sandbox, a queue that survives crashes, queries a hundred times faster, a pipeline that rolls itself back. None of it counts in an interview until you can tell it, clearly, in about ninety seconds, and answer the question that follows. Today you write the six stories you’ll tell, and test them against the rubric an interviewer carries in their head.

Why this matters

Big-tech and consulting interviews (BCG X included) have a behavioural part: “tell me about a time when…”. Strong engineers fail it by rambling, by saying “we” for ten minutes, or by never reaching a result. It’s a skill, so it’s trainable, and you’ve already lived the material.

1. STAR, and where the time goes

STAR is the shape interviewers listen for. Amazon’s interview guide describes it directly: the situation, what you were trying to accomplish, “what did you specifically do”, and how it turned out (Amazon: the interview loop). In 90 seconds:

PartTimeWhat it answers
Situation~10 sWhere, when, what was wrong. One or two sentences.
Task~10 sYour goal, and your role in it.
Action~45 sWhat you did, step by step, and why each step. The evidence of how you work.
Result~15 sWhat changed, with a number.
Learned~10 sWhat you’d do differently, or now always do.

2. Four habits that make a story land

  • “I”, not “we”. They’re hiring you. Credit the team, then say exactly what you did. “We migrated the database” leaves them guessing whether you led it or watched.
  • Numbers. “Much faster” is an opinion; “from 2,750 pages read to 23” is a fact they can write down. CodeArena is full of them: your lab outputs, your docs/perf/ plans, your attack suite.
  • Decisions, with their trade-offs. The follow-up is almost always “why that, and not X?”. Your ADRs are the answers: a story you understand bends under questions; a memorised one breaks.
  • Spoken English. Short sentences, one idea each. Cut filler (“basically”, “kind of”, “a lot of stuff”): it fills the air and empties the meaning.

The Tech Interview Handbook has a good, free guide with sample answers and the most common questions (behavioural interviews). Most questions map onto a handful of stories: a hard technical problem, a conflict, a failure, a decision with a trade-off, learning something fast.

3. Your six

  1. The sandbox: running strangers’ code safely; the walls, and the attack tests that prove each one.
  2. The faster query: one of your six plans, before and after, and how you found the cause.
  3. The idempotent queue: at-least-once delivery, and how a duplicate judges once.
  4. A production bug: one your real users found on 21 November, from report to test to fix.
  5. A disagreement: a code review, a 42 group project, a design choice you argued for or against.
  6. A failure: something that went wrong because of you, and what you changed afterwards.
