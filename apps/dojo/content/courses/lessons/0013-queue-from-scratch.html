A judging run takes seconds, needs Docker, and can crash halfway. None of that can happen inside an HTTP request. So the API hands the work to a queue, and a worker picks it up. Today you build that queue yourself on Redis, find each way it can lose or duplicate a job, and close them one by one. After that, BullMQ is a library whose every option you understand.

Why this matters

Tomorrow you wire POST /submissions → BullMQ → worker. Every bug there is one of today’s: a job lost when a worker dies, a job judged twice, a job pointing at a row that doesn’t exist yet. “Why not Kafka?” is your ADR-008, and you’ll be able to answer it.

1. Why a queue

The API’s job is to answer fast. So POST /submissions validates the body, writes a PENDING row, puts a job on the queue, and answers 202 Accepted: “I have it, not done yet” (MDN). A worker, a separate process next to Docker, takes the job, judges it and writes the verdict. The client polls GET /submissions/:id (or gets pushed the result, lesson 20).

  • The job carries the id, never the code. The row is the truth; the queue holds pointers to work, in Redis’s memory.
  • The row is written before the job. The other order lets a fast worker take a job whose row isn’t there yet. That only fails under load.
  • The queue absorbs bursts. 500 submissions at once don’t make 500 containers; workers take jobs at the pace the machine allows. That’s back pressure, from your system-design reading.

2. A queue on Redis lists, and its first hole

A Redis list is a queue already: LPUSH adds on the left, RPOP takes from the right, oldest first. But Redis’s own documentation points out the hole: once a worker has taken a job with RPOP, the job exists only in that worker’s memory. If the worker crashes, the job is gone (LMOVE, “Pattern: reliable queue”).

The fix is one command. LMOVE waiting active RIGHT LEFT takes the job and parks it in a processing list, atomically. When the work is done, the worker removes it with LREM (the ack). If the worker dies first, the job is still in active, and recovery can put it back. And because LMOVE is atomic, two workers can never take the same job.

3. At least once, retries, dead letters

Recovery has a price. If a worker crashes after judging and before the ack, the job runs again. You can’t avoid that: from the queue’s side, “crashed before the work” and “crashed after it” look the same. So a crash-proof queue delivers at least once, and the work itself must make a second delivery harmless. That’s lesson 14.

Failures come in two kinds, as in lesson 9. An infrastructure failure (Docker unreachable, database down) deserves a retry, a limited number of times. After the last attempt, or for a failure no retry can fix, the job goes to a dead-letter list, where a person or an alert can look at it instead of it vanishing. A wrong answer is neither: it’s a verdict, and the job succeeded.

One more hole, for multiple workers: recovering every active job would steal jobs still being worked on. Real queues give each taken job a lease that the worker renews; a job whose lease expires counts as stalled. BullMQ does exactly this (stalled jobs). It’s today’s challenge.

5. What BullMQ gives you, and why not Kafka

  • BullMQ (on Redis): queue.add(name, data, { jobId, attempts, backoff }), a Worker with locks and stalled-job recovery, delays, priorities and events (BullMQ docs). jobId set to the submission id is your duplicate-id set.
  • Kafka is a replicated log: ordered partitions, consumers that track offsets, replay. It’s built for streams of events at very high volume. A judge needs per-job retries and acknowledgments for a few jobs a second, which is a job queue’s shape. The condition that would make you switch is your ADR-008.
