Before a language model sees your prompt, a tokenizer turns it into numbers. The algorithm behind GPT-style tokenizers is byte-pair encoding: start from bytes, and repeatedly merge the most frequent pair into a new token. It’s twenty lines of logic, it runs over every document a model is trained on and every request it serves, and it’s exactly the kind of tight byte loop Rust is for. Today you write it from scratch.

Why this matters

This is where the course meets the honest version of “Rust is the language of AI”. The models are trained and called from Python, but the fast parts underneath are often Rust: Hugging Face’s tokenizers library is a Rust core with Python, Node and WebAssembly bindings (its README). Lesson 41 puts your tokenizer behind import in Python; lesson 42 makes it parallel.

1. Why subwords, and why bytes

A vocabulary of whole words can’t cover every name, typo and new word. Characters cover everything but make sequences long, and a model’s cost grows with sequence length. Subword tokens sit between: frequent words become one token, rare ones split into a few pieces. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch brought BPE from compression (Gage, 1994) to neural translation for exactly this reason (ACL 2016, arXiv:1508.07909). GPT-2 then ran it on bytes rather than characters (Radford et al., 2019, §2.2): the 256 byte values are the base vocabulary, so any text in any language encodes, and no token is ever “unknown”.

2. Training, by hand

Take aaabdaaabac, the example Wikipedia uses. Count every adjacent pair, overlaps included: aa appears 4 times. Merge it into a new token, Z: ZabdZabac. Count again: Za and ab tie at 2. The lab breaks ties towards the smallest pair, here ab (bytes 97, 98) before Za (256, 97), so Y = ab: ZYdZYac. Then X = ZY: XdXac. Now no pair occurs twice, so training stops. The merges, in order, are the tokenizer.

tokens:  a a a b d a a a b a c      11 tokens
merge 1: (a, a)  → 256            Z a b d Z a b a c      9
merge 2: (a, b)  → 257            Z Y d Z Y a c          7
merge 3: (256, 257) → 258         X d X a c              5

Encoding new text replays the merges by rank: while some adjacent pair has been learned, merge the one learned earliest. So aaab becomes [258]. Decoding expands each token back into its bytes. Andrej Karpathy’s minbpe is the same algorithm in about a hundred lines of Python, written to be read: keep it open next to your Rust.

encode, decode

Build a HashMap<Pair, rank> from the merges once. Then loop: among the adjacent pairs that have a rank, take the lowest (filter_map then min), merge it, repeat until none is left. token_bytes is naturally recursive: a merged token is its two halves’ bytes, concatenated.
